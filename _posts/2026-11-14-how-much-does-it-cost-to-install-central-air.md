## How much does it cost to install central air with existing ductwork?

<p>With good ducts already in place, a typical 2.5 ton install costs about $6,800, with a sample range of $5,200 to $9,500. The calculator prices ductwork modifications at $350 to $750, with a typical cost of $500. So how much does central air cost to install when the ducts are already there? About $6,800 for a typical home, because the ducts are the part you are not paying to build.</p>
<p>Homeowners often ask how much it costs to install a central air unit when the ducts are already there. The answer is the standard install price, but good ducts do not guarantee a simple job. The installer should still inspect them first, because leaky, undersized, or crushed ducts need repair, and a duct system that is too small for the new airflow will not cool the home well. Ask whether the quote includes a duct leakage test and a written price for any duct repair.</p>

## How much does it cost to install central air with no ductwork?

<p>The costs to install central air conditioning rise fastest when a home has no ducts. A home with no ducts needs a full duct install on top of the $5,200 to $9,500 range for a typical 2.5 ton system. With new ductwork, the calculator prices the job at $7,500 to $16,000. The ducts need supply runs, return airflow, and room to route through closets, attics, or finished ceilings, which is why the cost swings so much from home to home.</p>
<p>If there is no clear route for ducts, a ductless mini-split can be cheaper, because it needs no ductwork at all. Compare the full installed price of each option, not just the equipment price, and ask each installer to list ductwork as its own line.</p>

## SEER2 minimums, refrigerant, and the $5,000 rule

<p>Since January 2023 the federal minimum is 13.4 SEER2 in the North and 14.3 SEER2 in the Southeast and Southwest for systems under 45,000 BTU, per the US Department of Energy. New systems use R-454B or R-32 refrigerant, and the indoor coil is replaced with the condenser because the coil must match the new refrigerant and the new SEER2 rating.</p>
<p>Use the $5,000 rule before you pay for a repair, per iBuyer: multiply the age of the system in years by the cost of the repair, and replace it if the answer is over $5,000. Worked math: 12 years x $500 repair = $6,000, which is over $5,000, so replace. The same $500 repair on a 5 year old system gives 5 x $500 = $2,500, so repair it.</p>
<p>Do not count on the federal 25C tax credit: it ended December 31, 2025, under Public Law 119-21, per the IRS. Check state and utility rebates instead.</p>

## What affects the price

### Home size

Bigger homes need bigger systems, and bigger systems cost more. A 2,500 sq ft home needs about twice the cooling power of a 1,000 sq ft home, so the price rises with the size of the home.

### System size and SEER rating

System size is measured in tons, and the SEER rating measures efficiency. A higher SEER system costs about 15 to 30 percent more up front, but it uses less power and can cost less to run each summer.

### Ductwork condition

Good ducts in the right size can stay, but leaky, undersized, or damaged ducts need repair or replacement. Duct work is one of the biggest cost swings in a central air job, and it is often found only after the installer inspects the home.

### Location and labor rates

Labor rates vary across the USA, and big cities usually cost more than small towns for the same install. Hot states also have more demand in summer, which can push prices up in the busy season.

### Electrical panel

An older home may need a panel upgrade or a new circuit for the outdoor unit. That work is usually priced apart from the AC install, and it can add a large amount to the total job.

## How to save money

<ul class="checklist">
<li><strong>Get 3 written quotes.</strong> Compare the same system size, the same SEER rating, and the same scope, not just the bottom line price.</li>
<li><strong>Size it correctly.</strong> An oversized system costs more to buy and short cycles, which wastes power and wears the system out faster.</li>
<li><strong>Compare SEER payback.</strong> A higher SEER system costs more now, so ask each installer to show the yearly power saving before you pay the extra.</li>
<li><strong>Schedule in the off season.</strong> Fall and winter installs are often cheaper, because installers have fewer jobs booked.</li>
<li><strong>Check rebates, not the old federal tax credit.</strong> The federal 25C tax credit ended December 31, 2025, so check state and utility rebates for an efficient system before you buy.</li>

## How to hire an AC installer

<ol class="steps">
<li><strong>Ask for a load calculation.</strong> A good installer measures the home, checks insulation and windows, and sizes the system from the numbers, not from a guess.</li>
<li><strong>Get 3 written quotes.</strong> Ask each installer for the same system size and SEER rating, with ducts, permits, and warranty listed in writing.</li>
<li><strong>Verify license and insurance.</strong> Check the HVAC license for your state, and ask for proof of liability insurance and workers compensation cover.</li>
<li><strong>Compare warranties.</strong> Check the warranty on the equipment, the labor warranty, and what is needed to keep each warranty valid.</li>
<li><strong>Get everything in writing.</strong> The contract should list the brand, the model, the size, the SEER rating, the price, the start date, and the warranty terms.</li>

## Replacement vs a new install

<p>Replacing an old system costs less than a full new install with new ducts, but the scope is different. A typical new 2.5 ton install costs about $6,800, with a sample range of $5,200 to $9,500, and the calculator prices ductwork modifications at $350 to $750, with a typical cost of $500. A home with no ducts at all needs a full duct install on top of that range. Compare the scope line by line before you compare the totals: a replacement quote that leaves out ducts is not the same job as an install that includes them.</p>

## The $5,000 rule, worked twice more

<p>The $5,000 rule says to multiply the age of the system in years by the cost of the repair, and replace it if the answer is over $5,000. The guide already works two cases: 12 years times a $500 repair is $6,000, so replace, and 5 years times $500 is $2,500, so repair. Two more cases show the line. An 8 year old system with a $500 repair gives 8 times $500, which is $4,000, so repair it. A 10 year old system with a $600 repair gives 10 times $600, which is $6,000, so replace it. The rule is a guide, not a quote: a system that breaks down often, or one that uses the old refrigerant, can be worth replacing even when the rule says repair.</p>

## SEER2 payback, worked

<p>A higher SEER2 system costs about 15 to 30 percent more than a standard system. On the typical $6,800 system, 15 percent more is $6,800 times 1.15, which is $7,820, and 30 percent more is $6,800 times 1.30, which is $8,840. The extra $1,020 to $2,040 buys lower power bills each summer, so ask each installer to show the yearly power saving before you pay the extra. A higher SEER2 system also needs the indoor coil replaced with the condenser, because the coil must match the new refrigerant and the new SEER2 rating. Since January 2023 the federal minimum is 13.4 SEER2 in the North and 14.3 SEER2 in the Southeast and Southwest for systems under 45,000 BTU, per the US Department of Energy, so a legal install already meets that floor.</p>

## Install day: what happens

<p>Most installs take 1 to 2 days. A simple swap of an old system can take 1 day, while a job that needs new ducts or electrical work can take 2 days or more. The crew removes the old outdoor unit and indoor coil, sets the new condenser and coil, runs refrigerant lines and a drain line, connects the ducts, and fits the thermostat. Then they charge the system, start it, and test the cooling in each room. Clear the path to the indoor unit and the outdoor unit before the crew arrives, keep pets out of the work area, and plan for the cooling to be off during the work. Ask the installer to show you the new thermostat settings and the filter location before they leave.</p>

## How to read an AC quote

<p>A good quote lists the same items on every bid, so you can compare them fairly. Check that each quote lists the system size in tons, the SEER2 rating, the brand and model, the refrigerant type, and the price. Check whether ducts, permits, and the thermostat are included, and check the warranty on the equipment and on the labor. A quote that lists a 2.5 ton system at $6,800 with ducts included is not the same job as a quote that lists the same system at a lower price with ducts left out. The calculator prices ductwork modifications at $350 to $750, with a typical cost of $500, and a home with no ducts needs a full duct install on top of the $5,200 to $9,500 range, so a low quote that leaves out ducts can end up costing more than a dearer quote that includes them. Ask each installer to list ducts, permits, and warranty in writing before you sign.</p>

## Keeping a new system running

<p>A central air system lasts 15 to 20 years with regular care. Yearly service, clean filters, and a clean outdoor unit all help it last longer and run cheaper. Change the filter on the schedule in your manual, keep plants and leaves clear of the outdoor unit, and book a yearly service before summer, when installers are busiest. A system that short cycles, makes new noises, or cools unevenly is worth a service call early, because a small fault caught early usually costs less than a breakdown in a heat wave. Keep the warranty papers and the service records together, because some warranties need proof of yearly service to stay valid.</p>

## Warning signs your home needs extra work

<p>A quote can grow after the installer inspects the home. These signs point to extra work, so flag them before you accept a price. A new condenser cannot fix a badly designed duct system, and an install that starts before the ducts and electrics are checked can end with a dearer system that still cools unevenly.</p>
<ul class="checklist">
<li><strong>Several rooms have very weak airflow.</strong> The ducts may be undersized, crushed, or blocked.</li>
<li><strong>Ducts have visible tears, or flexible ducts are badly crushed.</strong> Leaky ducts waste cooled air before it reaches the rooms.</li>
<li><strong>Some rooms have never heated or cooled evenly.</strong> The duct layout may not suit the home.</li>
<li><strong>The electrical panel has very little spare capacity.</strong> A new circuit or a panel upgrade may be needed.</li>
<li><strong>Return air openings look small for the home.</strong> Poor return airflow strains the system and hurts comfort.</li>
<li><strong>Major duct runs pass through finished areas with no easy access.</strong> Repairs there cost more in labor and finish work.</li>
<li><strong>The home has no clear route for new supply ducts.</strong> A first-time install may need major construction to route them.</li>
</ul>

## Before you get quotes: a quick home survey

<p>You can do a simple home survey before you meet an installer. It will not replace a pro load calculation, but it makes your first quote far more useful, because the installer can see at once whether the home has usable ducts and electrics.</p>
<ul class="checklist">
<li><strong>Record the home size.</strong> Note the cooled floor area in square feet.</li>
<li><strong>Check whether the home uses forced-air heating.</strong> A furnace with ducts means the home already has a distribution system.</li>
<li><strong>Count the supply registers in each room.</strong> Note any room with no register at all.</li>
<li><strong>Find the return grilles.</strong> Note where they are and whether they look small for the home.</li>
<li><strong>Look at accessible ducts.</strong> Check for tears, crushed flexible sections, and falling insulation.</li>
<li><strong>Note rooms that run warmer than others.</strong> Uneven rooms point to duct or airflow problems.</li>
<li><strong>Take a photo of the indoor equipment label.</strong> The model and age help the installer size the replacement.</li>
<li><strong>Photograph the electrical panel from a safe distance.</strong> The installer can see whether there is spare capacity.</li>
</ul>
<p>Do not remove duct panels or the electrical panel cover, and do not enter an unsafe attic. Leave that inspection to the installer.</p>

## Sizing by BTU: check the tonnage yourself

<p>System size is measured in tons, and 1 ton equals 12,000 BTU per hour. A rough way to check the size is 20 BTU per square foot of interior space: multiply the home size by 20 to get the BTU rating, then divide by 12,000 to get tons. A 1,500 sq ft home needs about 30,000 BTU, which is 2.5 tons, and that matches the table on this page. A 2,000 sq ft home needs about 40,000 BTU, which is about 3.3 tons, close to the 3.5 ton system in the table. A 2,500 sq ft home needs about 50,000 BTU, which is about 4.2 tons, close to the 4 ton system in the table. This is a rough guide only, and a pro should confirm it with an ACCA Manual J load calculation, which looks at insulation, windows, and local climate. An oversized system costs more to buy and short cycles, which wastes power and wears the system out faster.</p>
