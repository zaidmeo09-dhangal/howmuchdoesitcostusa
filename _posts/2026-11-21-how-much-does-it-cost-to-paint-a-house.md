## Floor area vs wall area: read quotes the right way

<p>This calculator prices by home floor area, the square feet you enter. It does not price by paintable wall area. That matters because published per square foot exterior averages use floor area while per-surface figures use wall area, so the two never match. Wall area is larger than floor area, so a quote priced per wall square foot will show a lower rate per square foot for the same job. Compare totals, not per square foot rates, when you compare quotes. Ask each painter whether the quote prices floor area or wall area, and compare the totals for the same job.</p>

## What affects the price

### Home size

Larger homes need more paint and more labor hours, so size drives the total. A 3,000 sq ft home needs far more paint and time than a 1,000 sq ft home.

### Job type

Exterior jobs face weather and need tougher paint, while interior jobs need more care around furniture and floors. Exterior work also needs ladders and weather-safe days.

### Prep work

Cleaning, sanding, caulking, and minor repairs add time before any paint goes on. Homes with peeling paint or cracks need more prep than homes in good shape.

### Paint quality

Premium paint costs more per gallon and often covers better and lasts longer. A cheaper paint may need an extra coat, which can erase the saving. A gallon costs $20 to $30 on average, with a $10 to $100 range, per Angi.

### Number of coats

Two coats cost more than one and usually look better and last longer. A color change almost always needs two coats for full coverage. Double the gallons for two coats when you work out the paint quantity.

## Lead paint note

<p>Homes built before 1978 may contain lead paint, and federal law requires EPA RRP certification for renovation that disturbs lead paint. Tell your painter the age of the home before work starts. Lead paint removal is not included in a standard paint quote, so get it priced apart if it applies to your home.</p>

## When to repaint, and how long it takes

<p>Repaint your house exterior every 5 to 10 years, by climate and paint quality. Harsh sun, rain, and salt air shorten the life, while a mild climate and a good paint stretch it. A paint job takes about 3 to 5 days for an average home. A home of 1,200 sq ft or less takes 2 to 3 days, a home of 2,000 sq ft takes 3 to 5 days, and a home over 3,000 sq ft takes 5 to 7 days. Plan the work for dry weather on an exterior job, because rain stops exterior painting and can push the schedule out.</p>

## Best time of year to paint a house

<p>Exterior painting needs dry, mild days, so spring and fall are the best seasons in most of the USA: the paint dries well, the crew can work without heat or cold slowing the job, and rain is less likely to stop work. Summer heat and strong sun dry paint too fast on the surface, and cold slows drying and can leave a weak finish. Interior painting is less tied to the season, because the work is inside, but the off season is still the best time to book: painters with open dates are often more flexible on price, so ask for off season dates when you get quotes. Whatever the season, do not start an exterior job when rain is expected before the paint has dried.</p>

## How long paint lasts, and how to make it last longer

<p>Exterior paint lasts 5 to 10 years, by climate and paint quality, and interior paint lasts about as long in low traffic rooms, while hallways, kitchens, and rooms used every day wear sooner. Sun baked walls fade first, coastal homes face salt air and moisture that speed up peeling, and a mild climate stretches the life of a good paint job. Two things stretch any paint job: thorough prep before the paint goes on, and simple upkeep after it. Wash the exterior once a year, touch up chips promptly before water gets under the paint, and repaint before the old coat fails, because painting over peeling paint only buys a short time.</p>

## How to read a quote: the 5 lines to check

<p>A quote is only comparable when it lists the same job, so check these 5 lines before you compare totals. First, the paint brand and the paint quality, because premium paint covers better and lasts longer. Second, the number of coats, because a color change almost always needs two coats for full coverage. Third, the prep items, and how sanding, patching, primer, and caulking are priced, and what counts as a repair. Fourth, the scope: which surfaces are included, walls only, walls and ceilings, or walls, ceilings, and trim. Fifth, the cleanup plan and the warranty on the work, in writing. A low quote that leaves out prep, a second coat, or the trim is not cheaper, it is a smaller job.</p>

## Walls only, or walls, ceilings, and trim?

<p>Scope is the biggest price swing inside a house, and it is easy to compare the wrong jobs. Walls only is the cheapest scope, because it covers the largest flat surfaces and nothing else. Adding ceilings adds labor for overhead work and for cutting in around light fixtures, and adding trim and baseboards adds slow, detailed work around doors, windows, and floors. Hard to reach areas, ornate trim, and cathedral ceilings that need scaffolding push the labor higher still. When you compare quotes, check that each one lists the same scope: walls only, walls and ceilings, or walls, ceilings, and trim. A low quote that covers walls only is not cheaper than a higher quote that covers walls, ceilings, and trim, it is a smaller job. Ask each painter to list the surfaces included, the number of coats, and the paint brand, so the quotes match.</p>

## Choosing a paint finish

<p>Finish decides how the paint looks and how it holds up, and the right finish depends on the room. Flat paint hides wall flaws best, but it marks easily and is hard to clean, so it suits ceilings and low traffic bedrooms. Eggshell has a soft sheen, hides flaws fairly well, and cleans better than flat, so it suits living rooms and bedrooms. Satin has a smooth sheen, resists moisture, and cleans well, so it suits kitchens, bathrooms, and hallways. Semi-gloss is the toughest and easiest to clean, so it suits trim, doors, and cabinets. Glossier finishes cost more due to their added durability, and they also show wall flaws more clearly, so the prep must be better. For an exterior job, 100 percent acrylic latex is the common USA choice, because it handles weather and lasts longer than cheaper paint.</p>

## DIY vs hiring a pro, side by side

<p>Doing interior prep yourself saves $700 to $1,800 in labor, and doing exterior prep yourself saves $500 to $1,000, per Angi. That is the saving from prep alone, not from painting the whole house yourself. A full DIY interior job saves the labor, which is 70 to 90 percent of the total, at $25 to $100 per hour, but it needs your time, your tools, and your skill with cutting in, rolling, and working on ladders. An exterior DIY job also needs ladders or scaffolding, and it carries safety hazards, especially on a 2 or 3 story home. Hiring a pro gives a smoother finish in less time, and the painter brings the equipment, the skill, and the warranty. If you DIY, do the moving and light prep yourself and leave the high work and the finish coats to a pro when the job is large or high.</p>
